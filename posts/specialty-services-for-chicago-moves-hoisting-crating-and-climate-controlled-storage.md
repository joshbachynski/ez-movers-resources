Most Chicago moves are routine, and most Chicago movers can complete a routine job adequately. The category divides sharply when the job stops being routine. A nine-foot sectional that must enter a third-floor Lincoln Park walkup with a 32-inch landing turn is no longer a labor problem. It is an engineering problem. Hoisting is the clearest example. Chicago has thousands of brownstones, greystones, two-flats, and converted walkups where the original architects did not contemplate modern furniture sizes. A standard L-shaped sectional, an oversized king mattress with a non-flexing box spring, or a marble dining table will not turn the stairwell of a typical Chicago walkup. The two options are to disassemble the piece, which is sometimes impossible without destroying it, or to hoist the piece up the exterior of the building through a window or balcony. EZ Movers carries the rigging equipment, harnesses, and trained hoist crew to do this safely. The job requires reading the building, identifying the load-bearing window frames, planning the rope angles, and coordinating ground-level spotters. A Chicago moving company that does not regularly perform hoists will either decline the job or attempt a stair carry that ends in damage to the piece, the wall, or the railing.

Fine art crating is the second specialty that separates serious Chicago movers from the rest. A framed painting, a sculpture, or a mounted photograph cannot travel pad-wrapped on a moving truck. It needs a custom-built wooden crate sized to the piece, with internal foam blocking that immobilizes the work, vapor barriers for climate-sensitive materials, and external markings that prevent the crate from being stacked or inverted. EZ Movers builds these crates in-house for Chicago move clients moving art collections, gallery inventory, or single high-value pieces. The alternative offered by generic Chicago movers, which is to wrap the piece in moving blankets and hope, is the wrong tool for art that has any meaningful value.

Antique handling overlaps with both. A 19th-century armoire, a Victorian glass-front bookcase, or a mid-century rosewood credenza requires disassembly knowledge that newer furniture does not. The joinery is different, the finishes are more vulnerable, and the structural assumptions are not the same. EZ Movers' crews are trained to identify the construction type before applying any tool to the piece, which is the difference between an antique arriving intact and an antique arriving with a cracked veneer or a separated joint that cannot be repaired without specialty restoration.
